Case 04 · AI animation production system

Sama’s Sleepytime Stories

I created a children’s animation channel and turned my episode-making process into a reusable AI-assisted production system—from character direction through final edit.

The creative goal

Build a world that can return.

Sama’s Sleepytime Stories began as more than a single animated short. I wanted a repeatable world: familiar characters, a recognizable opening, complete episodes, and a process that could be used again without rebuilding every decision from zero.

My film and animation background gave me the directing framework—story rhythm, composition, performance, continuity, sound, and edit. Generative tools expanded what one creator could attempt, but they did not remove the need for taste or correction.

The real design challenge was consistency: keeping characters recognizable, scenes purposeful, and the final episode emotionally coherent across many separate generations.

The pipeline

Direction at every stage.

  1. Episode concept and structureDefine the lesson, emotional arc, scene order, and bedtime tone before generating assets.
  2. Character and asset directionTrain and refine recurring OpenArt characters, then generate the visual material needed for the intro and episode scenes.
  3. Motion and performanceUse tools including Kling and WAN to turn directed stills into usable movement, then reject or repair generations that misunderstand the action.
  4. Voice and editBuild narration and sound with ElevenLabs, then assemble pacing, timing, continuity, and final delivery in CapCut.
  5. Reusable assistantsCreate Claude-based helpers that prepare prompts and repeatable production steps while keeping final review and publishing manual.

What AI did—and did not do

Assistance is not authorship.

I was the sole human creator and director, not the only creative force involved. AI systems generated and transformed visual, motion, voice, and prompt material under my direction.

The assistants do not autonomously produce and publish a finished episode. They help reproduce my preparation process: organizing steps, drafting prompts, and reducing the effort required to start the next production cycle.

That distinction matters. The portfolio value is not “one-click animation.” It is the ability to design a workflow, direct multiple specialized tools, recognize failures, and assemble scattered outputs into one intentional episode.

Turning taste into instructions

The workflow carries my decisions forward.

A repeatable process only becomes useful when it records the decisions that usually stay in a creator’s head. For this project, that meant translating visual taste into practical instructions: how a character should read, which details must remain consistent, what kind of motion fits the moment, how long a shot should breathe, and what signals that a generated result needs to be rejected.

I used failed generations as production data. If a motion model misread a rope, changed an aspect ratio, or pulled the image toward a generic feature-animation look, the next prompt or checklist needed to address that failure. The process became stronger when the mistake left a paper trail instead of disappearing inside another round of trial and error.

The Claude assistants support that paper trail. They organize the sequence, prepare prompt drafts, and make the next episode easier to begin. They do not replace my review. The human work remains choosing, correcting, connecting, and deciding when many separate assets finally feel like the same story.

Production evidence

A system that produced episodes.

The project produced a channel identity, intro animation, recurring characters, scene assets, edited episodes, and a documented production approach. The channel currently demonstrates craft and repeatability rather than audience scale.

The honest result

Workflow proof before audience proof.

At the time of review, the channel had five subscribers and 51 total views. I do not describe it as an audience-growth success.

The production also revealed visible tool failures: motion could misread an action, aspect ratios could drift, and a model’s default “animated film” look could override my direction. Those failures became part of the workflow—review, reject, repair, and document.

The next improvement is quality control before scale: tighten the visual bible, formalize rejection criteria, and make the episode checklist strong enough that another collaborator could understand how the world is supposed to look and feel.

What this case proves: episodic direction, multimodal AI fluency, workflow design, visual quality control, and the discipline to turn experiments into a repeatable creative practice.
