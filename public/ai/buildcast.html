Case 01 · Client product development

BuildCast

I translated another person’s idea into a working product: clarifying the user experience, directing an AI-assisted build, testing the workflows, and preparing the result for DevOps handoff.

The brief

Make the idea usable.

The starting point was a client concept for helping construction professionals capture their work and turn it into useful visual content. The opportunity was clear, but the product experience was not yet defined.

My job was not simply to decorate screens or execute a fixed specification. I had to interpret what the client meant, decide what the user would actually do, and turn that understanding into a coherent product someone could test.

That meant balancing two audiences: busy professionals documenting active jobs, and the client who needed a product that felt understandable enough to explain, demonstrate, and eventually hand to an engineering team.

What I owned

Direction across the whole experience.

I worked as the product interpreter and creative decision-maker. Claude helped generate implementation, but the responsibility for what the product should do—and whether the output matched that intention—remained mine.

  1. Product definitionConverted conversations and goals into features, user flows, priorities, and a product vocabulary.
  2. Experience and interaction designShaped navigation, project setup, milestone capture, shot guidance, timelapse creation, portfolio presentation, and brand settings.
  3. AI-assisted build directionPrompted, reviewed, corrected, and iterated on the implementation instead of treating generated code as automatically correct.
  4. Testing and handoffWalked through workflows, logged gaps, clarified deployment needs, and separated prototype completeness from production readiness.

How I worked

Build, inspect, correct, repeat.

I treated each generated implementation as a proposal, not an answer. A feature began with the intended user behavior: what the person knows at that moment, what action they need to take, what feedback proves the action worked, and what should happen next. I then used Claude to help implement that slice and reviewed the result in the running product.

When a screen looked complete but the workflow broke, I returned to the underlying decision instead of patching the appearance. That loop exposed missing states, unclear labels, navigation problems, and moments where the system expected too much knowledge from the user.

I also kept a boundary between product direction and production infrastructure. I could shape, test, and demonstrate the experience, but I did not claim that a prototype deployment had solved security, payments, storage, monitoring, or operational scale. Capturing those gaps was part of the work because an honest handoff is more valuable than a polished illusion.

Three product decisions

The decisions behind the screens.

  1. Turn “make content” into guided capture.Instead of asking users to already know what to film, the Shot Guide connects common construction milestones to practical capture prompts. The product teaches the behavior it needs.
  2. Separate quick output from project memory.Timelapse creation can produce an immediate piece of content, while projects and milestones preserve the larger record. That keeps the tool useful for both one-off and ongoing work.
  3. Design for trust before polish.Bid Mode, brand settings, portfolio output, and admin controls were framed as parts of a professional workflow—not novelty features. The experience needed to feel dependable before it felt flashy.

What shipped

A testable product system.

The prototype supports timelapse creation across multiple project types and output lengths, guided shot capture, project and milestone organization, a portfolio view, brand controls, Bid Mode, and administrative workflows.

The work also exposed the difference between a convincing prototype and a production-ready system. Permanent storage, production secrets, Stripe webhooks, rate limits, background jobs, and monitoring were documented as engineering responsibilities rather than hidden behind presentation.

The honest result

Proof of translation—not fake traction.

BuildCast demonstrates that I can receive an idea I did not invent, find the product inside it, and stay with the work until there is something concrete to review and hand forward.

The public prototype contains some template-style marketing language and placeholder testimonials. Those are not evidence of real customers, adoption, or business results, so I do not use them as portfolio claims.

What this case proves: product interpretation, sustained iteration, cross-functional thinking, and the judgment to distinguish a working experience from a finished production system.
