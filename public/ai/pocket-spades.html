Case 02 · Original game + systems design

Pocket Spades

I designed a new game inside Spades: a risk-and-reward system that lets players stash tricks, upgrade their Pocket, and decide when to cash out a run.

The opportunity

Respect the game. Add a new decision.

Spades is culturally familiar, strategically deep, and emotionally charged. Most digital versions reproduce the same basic loop, then differentiate themselves through presentation or aggressive monetization.

I wanted to change the actual decision-making without stripping away what players already love. The Pocket mechanic creates a second layer: tricks can become immediate progress, stored value, or a gamble on a larger run.

The design question was not “How do I reinvent Spades?” It was “What new choice can live inside Spades and still feel like it belongs there?”

The system

Stash. Upgrade. Run.

The mechanic had to be explainable in three verbs and deep enough to reward judgment over time.

  1. StashPlayers can place earned tricks into their Pocket instead of treating every hand as an isolated score event.
  2. UpgradeAccumulated value changes what the Pocket can do, creating a visible progression path inside the match.
  3. RunPlayers choose when to convert the stored position into a higher-risk play. Waiting may increase the reward; it may also expose the player to loss.
  4. Pocket BooksThe system reframes sandbags as usable strategic material, connecting the new layer to a rule experienced players already understand.

What I owned

Rules, interface, behavior, and feel.

I originated the concept and directed the product across physical and digital forms. Early work included collaborating with a developer; later, AI-assisted development let me continue iterating on the game directly.

My responsibility spans the rule system, player-facing language, scoring logic, interface behavior, visual identity, test scenarios, and ethical monetization position. The rule is simple: never sell power, interrupt a hand with ads, or manufacture frustration through coin-depletion loops.

Because game systems can feel correct while still producing bad outcomes, I treated simulation and live-device testing as design tools—not just engineering chores.

From rule to software

Every edge case is a design question.

A tabletop rule can rely on people to interpret intent. Software cannot. Turning Pocket Spades into a digital game meant defining exactly when a Pocket changes, what the player is allowed to do, how the interface communicates risk, how scoring resolves, and what happens when multiple systems collide at the end of a hand.

I used AI-assisted development to work through those states, but I evaluated them as a game designer. When the build behaved unexpectedly, I traced the problem back to the rule, the interface language, or the implementation instead of accepting the first technical fix. Automated tests protected known behavior while simulated hands helped reveal patterns that were hard to see through casual play alone.

Real-device testing added another layer. I tested multiplayer behavior across desktop and iPhone because a game that works in one browser window is not yet proof of a shared experience. The goal was not merely to remove errors. It was to make the new mechanic readable enough that the technology disappears and the decision feels natural at the table.

Evidence

Test the mechanic, not just the mockup.

The current work includes a functioning React game, server behavior, automated tests, simulated hands, and real-device multiplayer checks. A separate Yank Spades variant is kept distinct so experiments do not blur the core Pocket identity.

Quality-control cases

Every bug clarifies the rule.

I treated failures as evidence, not embarrassment. Testing on real phones and at scoring boundaries exposed places where the interface, timing, or game rule was still ambiguous. Each fix became a clearer product decision—and, where possible, a test that protects it from returning.

01Observe the contradiction

Eleven sandbags were displayed as four Pocket Books, even though the written rule groups sandbags in sets of four.

02State the rule precisely

Eleven sandbags must resolve to two complete Pocket Books with three sandbags remaining.

03Protect the behavior

Correct the calculation and add a regression test so the same scoring error cannot quietly return.

What this proves

Original thinking can survive contact with a system.

Pocket Spades is more than a themed interface or a generated app. It demonstrates invention, systems thinking, patient iteration, and the ability to use technology to test an original creative thesis.

The product is still in development. The portfolio claim is not market success or a finished commercial launch. The claim is that I created a genuinely distinct mechanic, translated it into a playable experience, and built an evidence trail around how it behaves.

The through-line: invent the idea, make the rules legible, test the consequences, and protect the player experience while the product grows.
