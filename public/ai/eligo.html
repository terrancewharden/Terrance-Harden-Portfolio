Case 03 · Interactive AI storytelling

Eligo

I designed a voice-driven storytelling experience where people choose the direction, AI performs the narration, and controlled randomness keeps the story social and unpredictable.

The idea

Make AI storytelling feel shared.

Most AI story experiences begin with a blank prompt and ask the user to become the writer. Eligo starts from a different social question: what if a group could steer a story together without stopping the momentum to write it?

The product is built around listening, choosing, reacting, and occasionally disrupting the expected direction. Voice makes the interaction feel closer to a storyteller in the room. Branching choices give players agency without turning the experience into a text editor.

The result sits between an interactive audio story, a party game, and a collaborative imagination exercise.

Experience design

Control without killing surprise.

  1. Eleven genre entry pointsUsers begin with a recognizable tone instead of an empty prompt, reducing friction while keeping the experience broad.
  2. Branching choice momentsThe story pauses at designed decision points so the room can choose a direction and feel the consequence.
  3. Party ModeThe experience is framed for shared use—living rooms, gatherings, and car rides—not only solitary screen time.
  4. WildwordA controlled randomizer introduces unexpected material without surrendering the whole story to noise.
  5. Choice-point currencyA visible system gives decisions weight and creates pacing around when the audience can intervene.

Creative direction

A product is also a performance.

I treated narration, interface, pacing, and visual atmosphere as one experience. The parchment treatment was an intentional experiment: a way to test whether the interface could feel like an artifact rather than another generic chat window.

The work required judging more than whether the application technically ran. Did a choice arrive at the right time? Could people understand what to do without instruction? Did randomness create delight or break the story? Did the voice feel like part of the world?

Original stories were not represented as finished authored content. Some placements were intentionally reserved for future originals that I would write with AI assistance.

Designing with model behavior

Give the AI room—but give it rails.

Generative narration creates a tension between freedom and continuity. If the model is controlled too tightly, the experience feels prewritten and the player’s choice becomes cosmetic. If it is given unlimited freedom, characters, tone, and story logic can drift until the experience stops feeling intentional.

I approached that tension as an experience-design problem. Genre establishes expectations. Choice points constrain the next turn. Wildword introduces disruption through a specific doorway instead of randomizing everything. The interface makes the player’s available power visible, while the narration keeps the result moving forward as a performance.

Testing meant listening for failures that a normal interface checklist would miss: repetition, weak escalation, sudden changes in voice, a choice that did not meaningfully affect the scene, or a generated passage that was technically coherent but emotionally flat. My role was to shape the container around the model and judge the story that came out—not to pretend that the model’s output became authored work simply because I initiated it.

What I learned

A good reaction is not retention.

A small number of early users responded positively to Eligo, but they did not return consistently. Outreach did not produce replies. I do not present that as traction.

That result matters because it separates product delight from product habit. The experience can be interesting in the moment and still need a stronger reason to come back, a clearer repeat-use setting, or more authored content with a distinct voice.

The next responsible step is not to add features at random. It is to observe complete sessions, identify the moment enthusiasm fades, and test whether a more specific recurring context—such as group play, car rides, or episodic originals—creates a reason to return.

What this case proves: I can define an unconventional interaction model, combine narrative direction with AI behavior, ship the loop, and be honest when the next challenge is adoption rather than invention.
